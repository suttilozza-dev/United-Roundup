WHO’S SAYING WHAT

Is Michael Carrick the problem at Manchester United — or just the easiest thing to change?

Manchester United’s midweek collapse against Brighton followed a home derby defeat to Manchester City, and Michael Carrick is already fielding questions about pressure on camera. Reporters, pundits and Carrick himself are reading the same week very differently — from a squad-building problem years in the making to a question of physical conditioning under the current staff.

LS
Laurie SutcliffeFounder and Editor, United Roundup · 18 September 2026

Six games into his first full season as permanent head coach, Michael Carrick is being asked on camera whether he feels under pressure.

A home derby defeat and a two-goal lead thrown away against Brighton did that in four days. The argument now is not whether the week was bad — everyone agrees on that — but what it proves.

What has been confirmed?

United have four points from four Premier League matches: beaten at Hull, 5-2 winners over Ipswich, a 2-2 draw at Everton, and a 1-0 home derby defeat on 13 September to a City side that played with ten men for two thirds of the game.

On Wednesday, United led Brighton 2-0 inside ten minutes through Shea Lacey and Mason Mount and lost 3-2 — Charalampos Kostoulas pounced on the stroke of half-time after big Benjamin Sesko was muscled off the ball and United’s defence were caught out by the subsequent ball forward into the box, Pascal Gross and Maxim De Cuyper both netted inside five second-half minutes; 65’ & 70’ respectively. A second successive early exit from the competition, and the team was booed off. As the team entered the tunnel, chants that can’t be repeated here were heard from the Stretford End and South Stand as fans were questioning what they had just witnessed.

Carrick accepted responsibility publicly: “It’s on me ultimately: as a team what we look like, how we play.” United did beat Sabah 4-0 in the Champions League on 10 September but that result has quickly been overshadowed by two defeats the following week of what is a busy calendar of fixtures for the reds.

Fulham away is next as September’s final game before the International break. Then it’s on to five games in October, six in November and United potentially playing seven times in December (TBC). A run of games that will surely be a real test of their squad, its depth and fitness. And Carrick will be trying to get the best out of his men to turn a poor start into winning momentum.

Who is saying what?

Michael Carrick (the manager — primary source). He takes the blame and rejects the framing of a question put to him. “We had the game exactly where we wanted it and let it get away from us in a big way,” he said on Wednesday, and on the boos: “I totally understand the feelings of the supporters. We have to accept it.” At Thursday’s press conference he stated: “I don’t feel under pressure. Either way, that doesn’t bother me one bit.”

Graeme Bailey, TeamTalk (reporting). A club source told him there will be “no knee-jerk reactions here” and that the hierarchy still believe they have “a very good young head coach”. Bailey also reports the club privately accepts the summer window left gaps, no cover at left-back among them. The BBC has separately reported no sense that the hierarchy are losing faith. Both carry the same caveat: results are expected to improve.

Sam Blitz, Sky Sports (analysis). His verdict is that the tenure is heading one way. He cites Sky’s stat that this was the first time in 551 half-time leads United had lost at home after leading at the break — the previous such occasion was in 1984 — and points at squad-building rather than one night: roughly £365m on seven central midfielders since August 2022, with £70m Carlos Baleba watching from the stands. On the same coverage, Jamie Redknapp questioned conditioning: “They didn’t look fit enough.”

Supporter media (opinion). The mood inside Old Trafford is not in dispute: Sky’s live coverage recorded the crowd’s verdict in fairly direct terms, and fan channels treated the night as a reckoning — Stephen Howson’s reaction went out under the title “What was that?!” and other supporter channels mirrored his sentiment.

Where do they agree — and disagree?

Everyone, Carrick included, agrees the second half was indefensible, and nobody credible is reporting that a sacking is imminent.

The disagreement is about cause. Carrick and the club treat it as a bad week inside a longer project. Blitz treats it as the visible cost of four years of midfield recruitment, which no head coach fixes by Sunday. Redknapp and FootballTransfers’ Suraj Radia, who notes United rank in the bottom three for sprints and high-speed runs, locate it in physical output — which, unlike recruitment, is a coaching matter.

What remains unknown is the club’s threshold. “No knee-jerk reactions” is not a guarantee, and nobody has said how many more results like this it survives.

THE UNITED ROUNDUP VIEW

A bad week, not proof of anything yet

The evidence supports one firm conclusion: this is a bad run with a real structural backdrop, and the running numbers are the part of it that lands squarely on the coaching staff. Supporters are entitled to ask why a side that has spent what United have spent is among the least physically productive in the division, and to want that answered at Fulham rather than with a line about togetherness.

What cannot be claimed as fact just yet is that Carrick is on borrowed time. Every current report says the opposite, and single-source talk of a countdown is speculation dressed as insight. He has a matter of days to make Thursday’s confidence look earned.

EDITOR’S OWN VIEW — NOT FACT

The training ground doesn’t look like it used to

I remember seeing the footage that came out of the training ground back in the late 80s/early 90s and watching the likes of Pallister, Bruce, Ince, Hughes, Sharpe and Parker, then later, Cantona, Scholes, Ferdinand, Butt, Keane and our now manager Carrick himself. I remember the intensity of those training sessions and I’d think “Wow!”, those players on the whole have gone on to do interviews after retiring where they’d comment quite consistently that “training was tougher than the games”. Just let that sink in and now compare them to today’s training footage. I for one, see a vast difference in the way players treat their training ground time. After seeing players of Fergie’s era being super physical, throwing themselves into challenges in the rain and mud, you could see the importance of the application of each player, at all times. If they didn’t train as hard as they should, my guess is they heard about it. And because of that, cardio, tenacity and fight were never an issue under Sir Alex. How do you think “Fergie-time” was made? We didn’t let whole games slip away from us on a regular basis, that’s for sure!

What we have seen since is a serious and consistent decline, nay — a slump in the physical condition of Manchester United teams post 2013. And my thoughts are that due to the off field turmoil and restructuring that felt near constant at times, and the changes that obviously had to training staff, routines and conditions, standards have dropped, nowadays it feels half-arsed in comparison. Players look like training is a photo opportunity rather than exercise and trade-skills honing work. It is crazy to think twelve managerial changes since 2013 is just an average sized issue that the club as a whole have had to deal with throughout this entire period.

What I want to see going forward is a real wake-up and increase in intensity of training. They’re fighting for places in the team (apparently). The signing of Carlos Baleba will indeed bring a bit of physicality to the midfield but in the upcoming two or three transfer windows we need to see the Manchester United ownership actually take ownership and address clear issues in the squad. Speed, cardio, intensity, goal threat, supply into the forwards and solid defensive back line are all on the list of things to improve. I think the acquisition of players that embody what we actually want our squad to be is paramount. So far it’s either not enough from the hierarchy or too little too late. And to go on a start to put pressure on the manager on the back of that is, in my view, unfair.
