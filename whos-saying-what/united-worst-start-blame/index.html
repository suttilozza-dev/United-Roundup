WHO’S SAYING WHAT

Who’s carrying the blame for United’s worst-equalling start—and is Carrick actually under threat?

Five points from five league games ties United’s worst start since 2014-15. Four named voices put the blame in four different places, three lines of reporting say the club isn’t moving on Michael Carrick, and one claim about Antonio Conte is still unsettled.

LS
Laurie SutcliffeFounder and Editor, United Roundup · Friday 2 October 2026

Five points from five Premier League games ties United’s worst start to a season since 2014-15.

That much nobody disputes, including Michael Carrick, who called it a genuine problem himself back in August. What’s actually in dispute this week isn’t the record—it’s who’s responsible for it, and whether Tottenham’s visit to Old Trafford on Sunday is the moment that changes.

On both questions, we’ve gathered named people with bylines that are pointing in different directions, and at least one factual claim, underneath all of it, still hasn’t been settled.

What has been confirmed

United have five points from five league games this season—level with their 2014-15 campaign for the club’s joint-worst start at this stage (Sky Sports, 21 September). The season so far: lost 2-0 at Hull, beat Ipswich 5-2 at home, drew 2-2 at Everton, lost 1-0 at home to Manchester City, lost 3-2 at home to Brighton in the EFL Cup after leading 2-0, and drew 1-1 at Fulham. That’s 12th after five league games. The Brighton defeat was United’s first home loss after leading by two goals since Tottenham beat them 3-2 in 1976.

Carrick remains head coach and the club has issued no statement on his position either way. On the record during the summer window, he said: “We want more, we need more.”

Benjamin Šeško has withdrawn from Slovenia duty injured. His own statement: “Right now, the most important thing is to fully focus on my recovery.” Neither the club nor his national coach, Boštjan Cesar, has put a timeline on it—Cesar’s only description is that it’s “an atypical injury that requires time to heal.”

Next up: Tottenham at Old Trafford, 10 October.

What isn’t confirmed by the club: how long Šeško is out for, any decision on Carrick’s future, or any contact with another coach.

Who’s saying what

Four named voices, four different places to put the blame—and that’s really the story this week.

Fabrizio Romano says there’s no managerial movement and the dressing room isn’t the problem: “As of today, Man United have not made any movement to contact other coaches,” and “the large majority of the players are absolutely backing and supporting the ideas of Michael Carrick” (29 September).

Dale O’Donnell at United in Focus points at recruitment, arguing United made “a big mistake by not signing a back-up striker in the summer”—leaving Carrick to use Mbeumo and Cunha out of position once Šeško went down, and now facing an emergency fix in January. This is supporter opinion, not sourced reporting, and it leans on an unnamed-source Mateta link via TEAMtalk that we’re not treating as confirmation.

Laurie Whitwell of The Athletic, speaking on a podcast rather than in a written report, frames it as financial rather than a failure of nerve: a striker or left-back was affordable, but doing it “would have basically pushed them again closer to having to then qualify for the Champions League” under UEFA’s 70% spending threshold—tighter than the Premier League’s own 85% rule. Whitwell separately attributes the Glazers’ reluctance to pay down the club’s debt to the fact that doing so “would hit their bottom line.”

Rio Ferdinand, on his own show, goes straight at ownership: “My question would be: why have you not paid down the loan, so that we don’t have to pay this £60million interest per season that is hindering the football club?”

On the Spurs game itself, Alan Smith (Sky Sports, via Read Man Utd) calls it the pressure point without predicting an outcome: “I’m not saying he would get the sack, but there’d be so many questions levelled at him if they were to lose to Spurs at Old Trafford.”

Where sources agree—and disagree

The agreement is wider than the headlines suggest. Nobody—not Carrick, not any journalist quoted above—disputes that the start is bad. Nobody disputes the squad has a hole in it somewhere, even if they can’t agree which one. And on whether the club itself is preparing to move on Carrick, Romano, Whitwell and TEAMtalk all describe the same picture independently: no contact with replacement candidates, director Jason Wilcox described as favouring stability, and no internal appetite for a reactive change.

Where they genuinely split is over the cause. Gary Neville’s view from before this window was that application on the pitch is the issue, and that Carrick is wrong to defend the players publicly—this sits as background rather than part of this week’s reporting; it predates 24 September and Carrick has already rejected it on the record (“I didn’t think we did... we controlled a lot of the first half”). Inside the current window, the four-way split above (players and coaching, circumstance, recruitment, finance) is real and unresolved, and all four are held by named people rather than anonymous sources.

One factual dispute remains genuinely open underneath all of this: whether United made any contact with Antonio Conte. Calciomercato reported on 23 September that “some contact” had been made; TEAMtalk had already denied it on 21 September, and Romano’s 29 September line—the most recent word—denies it again. Neither side has a named, on-record source, so this stays unresolved rather than settled by whoever spoke most recently.

THE UNITED ROUNDUP VIEW

The decision is about money, not the manager—for now

The genuinely useful finding this week isn’t any one of the four blame theories—it’s that a club under this kind of pressure usually generates conflicting signals about whether change is coming, and this one hasn’t. Three independent, credible lines of reporting all say the hierarchy isn’t moving on Carrick. That’s worth taking seriously, not as reassurance that results don’t matter, but as evidence the decision United’s ownership is actually facing is the recruitment and financial one Whitwell and Ferdinand are describing, not an imminent managerial one.

What would change that assessment: a result at home to Tottenham bad enough to shift Wilcox’s stated patience, a named source standing behind either side of the Conte claim, or the club actually confirming what’s wrong with Šeško and for how long. None of that exists yet.

What comes next

Tottenham visit Old Trafford on 10 October. Carrick’s pre-match press conference, expected the week of 5 October, is the next point at which the striker question, the Conte question and the Šeško timeline could all be put to him directly—and the single most useful thing to watch before next week’s piece.
