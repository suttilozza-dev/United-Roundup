New Head Coach, Same Old Bosses

Fourth time lucky for Mauricio Pochettino — but will anybody at Old Trafford own up to actually picking him?

Well. We said last week this felt like a club waiting for a decision. This week somebody finally made one. Just not quickly, not quietly and, depending on which paper you read, not necessarily the people whose job it is to make it.

The weekly roundup news window started with a party. Forty years since Sir Alex Ferguson walked through the door as manager of Manchester United, a dinner at Old Trafford, legends in the room, and the chief executive on his feet talking about how Fergie "built a culture" [1]. A day or two later we were 2-0 down to Aston Villa inside four minutes, the goalkeeper had let one through his legs, and there was a banner in the Stretford End telling the owners they could keep their new stadium [2] and chants from the crowd telling them exactly where. We came back. Of course we came back. Not once, but twice. 4-2 down in stoppage time, 4-4 at the end, Patrick Dorgu heading in at the death while Unai Emery was already halfway down the tunnel [3][4]. Brilliant, ridiculous and, in the end, still not enough: 18th, one league win since August, the club's lowest position after ten games in 40 years [2].

Then came three days of the most public private process you will ever see. Carrick turned up at Carrington at quarter past seven in his training kit while the people deciding his future flew to Monaco to see Sir Jim Ratcliffe [5][6]. By Wednesday everyone knew. By Thursday morning he was gone, told face to face and out of the building just after ten, followed by a 142-word statement calling him a legend [7][8]. By Thursday night David Ornstein and Fabrizio Romano had Mauricio Pochettino on a two-and-a-half-year deal [9][10], and United have since confirmed him as head coach [11]. He takes charge once he has finished coaching the USA against Haiti.

Along the way Gary Neville called the handling "disgusting" [12], Rio Ferdinand said the statement read like ChatGPT [13], the Daily Mail reported that the Glazers had overruled INEOS on the appointment (United deny it) [14], and Bruno Fernandes turned down a second contract offer [15][16]. The man coming in is the man United came close to appointing three times before [17]. Whether he's the answer depends rather a lot on whether the question was ever really about the head coach.

How to read this. It runs in the order things happened: the week before Villa, the game, the limbo, the sacking, Pochettino, then the questions it all leaves. Every claim is labelled. Numbers in square brackets point to the sources at the bottom, where the label for each one sits. The opening above and The United Roundup view at the end are my own words, so they carry no labels.

Who is saying what?

Eight talking points from the week, in the order they happened, then what comes next. The Big Story sits in the middle, where it happened.

1. Ferguson at 40, and the week before Villa

Forty years on from 6 November 1986, the club, the old players and half the press box all marked the day [18][19]. Henry Winter called him the "greatest manager of the Premier League era" [19]. The dinner that followed is where this week's first irony sits. According to Ducker and Burt in The Telegraph, Carrick was "on great form" in the room, and Berrada gave a speech listing Ferguson's virtues and how he "built a culture" [1]. Within the week Berrada was the man telling Carrick he was finished [24]. The Telegraph adds that several people in the room, and plenty of fans beyond it, felt INEOS had taken a sledgehammer to that culture [1]. The Mail says eyebrows were raised [7].

On the football side, Carrick's press conference brought bad news on Baleba. "Carlos, we don't know exactly, but it will be a number of weeks," he said, and he would not be back over the international break [20]. Dorgu was back in training [21]. UEFA confirmed Matheus Cunha's ban for the Roma red card stays at one match, so he misses only Sporting away: "We are not investigating further this matter" [33]. No United player made Thomas Tuchel's England squad, Kobbie Mainoo included [34].

The Athletic's Conor O'Neill put the whole season into one line: 167 league shots, the most in the division, and 10 goals [35][36]. Forty-five per cent of those shots come from outside the box, at 0.08 xG a shot [35]. For comparison, City had 161 shots and 33 goals at the same stage of 2022-23 [35]. Philip Buckingham noted November had arrived without a Premier League sacking, and Carrick was still the bookmakers' favourite to be the first [37].

Hanging over all of it were Avram Glazer's remarks that United "are not dependent on success on the pitch for commercial power" [38]. Former chairman Martin Edwards replied that supporters "want to hear that you're winning trophies" [38]. Teddy Sheringham called it "disgusting" [39]. The Athletic's Chris Weatherspoon, going through the accounts, found a £47m pre-tax loss alongside record EBITDA and wrote that the Glazers "cast a lengthening shadow" [40][41].

2. United 4-4 Aston Villa (Sun 8 Nov)

Fifty-nine seconds in, Alysson's shot went through Senne Lammens' legs, with Diogo Dalot failing to close him down [3][42]. At three minutes 40 seconds Lammens parried another Alysson shot straight to Nicolas Jackson. The BBC's Simon Stone says that was the earliest United have ever been 2-0 down in a Premier League game [2].

Opta says it is the first Premier League game in which a side has twice come back from two goals down to draw [3][48]. By the numbers United were the better side: 26 shots to 12 and xG of about 3.55 to 2.88 [49][45]. The Guardian's Tim de Lisle added the necessary footnote. Villa "won 2-1 on hapless episodes in their own box, and 2-0 on ridiculous starts to a half" [45]. The table was less forgiving: 18th, eight points from ten games, and the only league win the 5-2 against Ipswich on 30 August [2][3].

The goalkeeper and the left-back. Sky's co-commentators went straight for the two men in the firing line. Ashley Young said that since his World Cup mistake Lammens "hasn't looked the same as last season", and on Dalot for the opener: "He's got to slide in" [42]. Roy Keane: "The keeper's got to do better" [42]. Simon Stone's ratings had Lammens and Dalot on 4 and Dorgu on 8, "What an ending" [46]. The Guardian's Will Unwin called Dalot a "pantomime villain" but made the fairer point: he is a right-back playing left-back because nobody found cover for Luke Shaw, "merely a scapegoat" for a defence that has conceded 30 goals in all competitions [44]. Unwin notes the chants were aimed at Wilcox [44].

Carrick's best afternoon of the season, and his last. The MEN's Tyrone Marshall argued Carrick out-thought the man touted as his replacement. The triple change worked, and Emery "took off all four goalscorers" [50]. Pat Nevin wrote in his BBC column that Carrick stands amid the mayhem "trying – usually succeeding – in exuding an air of calm. This man deserves an Oscar" [51]. Emery went down the tunnel before the end. "I think the match was finishing," he said afterwards [4]. Villa's Damian Vidagany complained that Lammens "went straight to make contact with his hip on Zion", and "the rules are the rules" [47]. McGinn's verdict: "We lost two points. We threw it away" [52]. Keane, asked about Suzuki: "football is a contact sport" [53].

The mood. BBC Sport's feature on the week opens with a message sent to someone close to Carrick at 4-2 down: "Be gentle" [54]. At full time Carrick walked round the pitch in the middle of his players so the crowd would not turn on them, as it had after Bournemouth [54]. Stone notes the players have not downed tools for him [2]. Dion Dublin said they were walking round afterwards "like they had lost" [2]. Dorgu: "Everyone needs to look at themselves and ask if they are doing enough. We need to fight for the manager" [2]. Mount: "as players we have to front it" [55]. Asked on Sky whether he would walk away, Carrick said: "That's not going to happen" [56][2]. Told there would "clearly be conversations" with the hierarchy, he replied: "You're telling me what you think" [2].

The crowd was angry, though not with him. A banner in the second tier of the Stretford End said "We don't want a new stadium, we want new owners" in the BBC and Telegraph versions, while PA has "...we want the owners out" [2][1][57]. There were green-and-gold balloons, and Berrada and Wilcox were shouted at in the directors' box [43][57]. The 1958 had billed it as "the start of a sustained and diverse campaign" and later accused the club of "censorship" over confiscated banners [58][59]. Gary Neville said on NBC that the defenders who finished the game "will get you relegated" [23].

3. Three days in limbo (9–11 Nov)

By Monday night the story wasn't whether, it was when. The Athletic reported United expected to make a change before Anfield, with work on replacements already started [23]. The Guardian reported Wilcox and Berrada had concluded he should go [60]. BBC Sport reported the club wanted to tell him face to face [6]. Then came the scheduling. talkSPORT, the Sun and the Guardian had the Monaco meeting on Tuesday; BBC Sport, Sky and the MEN said Wednesday [23][25][60][6][26]. It was Wednesday. The BBC says it was a regular meeting, not one called specially [6].

In the meantime the head coach went to work. Carrick drove into Carrington at 7.15am on Tuesday in his training kit, cameras at the gate, and took a squad thinned by international duty on Tuesday and Wednesday [5][24]. Nick Miller in The Athletic described the "liminal space between employment and dismissal" and asked the obvious question: "Guys… Zoom?" [61]. In his view the decision should have come weeks ago, and he noted United would be paying about $10m for a manager they "could, theoretically, have had for free in the summer" [61]. The club's line, through unnamed sources to The Telegraph, was that leaks were "frustrating" but it "had to continue to follow due process" [27].

Sky gave three reasons on Tuesday for why it had not been rushed: the fans were not calling for him to go, the players were behind him, and there were doubts a change would fix anything [26]. Kaveh Solhekol went further: "You could have Carlo Ancelotti, Pep Guardiola or Jurgen Klopp. But with the present ownership structure, I don't think things are going to change" [26].

Pochettino moved to favourite within a day. The Sun had his odds cut from 25-1 to 2-1 [25]. ESPN and The Athletic reported a release clause of about $10m in the USA contract he had extended to 2030 in August [62][23]. Sky said he was open to talks [26]. By Wednesday the Guardian reported a framework agreement with Pochettino before the decision on Carrick had even been ratified [28].

The criticism started before the sacking did. Neville, on Stick to Football, said it felt "a little bit clumsy" and "a little bit classless", adding: "you can't leave a guy hanging for 24 or 48 hours just because you live in Monaco" [63]. Peter Schmeichel called it "very disrespectful" and said "we should stick with Michael" [64]. Norway coach Ståle Solbakken, asked about it at a press conference, said that if it was true, it was "probably not the first mistake Ratcliffe and co have made" [65]. Marshall's MEN column said Ratcliffe and "his band of yes men" have made United worse: since Ratcliffe took over football operations Ten Hag lasted 31 games, Amorim 63, Carrick 32 [66]. The Sun, citing talkSPORT's Ben Jacobs, said Carrick's contract had a break clause that made sacking him cheap [67].

4. The Big Story, part one: Michael Carrick sacked (Thu 12 Nov)

WHAT IS CONFIRMED AND REPORTED

Carrick arrived at Carrington at 7.50am, this time not in training kit. He was told by Berrada (with Wilcox, according to most outlets) and left shortly after 10.15am [7][27][24]. The Mail says the first reports broke at 9.30am and United called them premature until the statement at 10.37am [7]. The statement itself: "Manchester United can confirm that Michael Carrick has departed his role as men's first-team head coach." It calls the decision an "extremely difficult conclusion", says he "is, and always will be, a Manchester United legend", and promises a permanent successor "as quickly as possible" [8][68].

Assistant Steve Holland and coaches Jonathan Woodgate and Jonny Evans left with him; the Mail and Sky add Travis Binnion and goalkeeper coach Craig Mawson [24][27][7][69]. The Mail says Evans left on Wednesday night with his car "piled high with cardboard boxes" [7]. The under-21s coach Adam Lawrence took Thursday's session [7].

The numbers, as the club statement and the reporting have them: 12 wins from 17 and third place last season [8]. This season, three wins from 15 in all competitions and one in the league [27][69]. Fifteen wins in 32 overall [69]. He lasted under six months of the two-year contract he signed in May [54][27]. The Telegraph says it is the first time in the club's 148-year history that United have sacked two managers in one calendar year [27], and ESPN's Rob Dawson notes it is a mid-season change for the third season running [70]. Club sources told The Telegraph and ESPN that one league win in ten was "not acceptable" and that "our playing squad is far better than performances and results so far this season have indicated" [27][70].

WHO IS SAYING WHAT ABOUT HOW IT WAS DONE

This is as near to a consensus as the week got. Neville on Sky: "Indecision, lack of authority, just basically really poor leadership… It's just quite simply disgusting." Anywhere else, he said, "it would be constructive dismissal and you'd be getting sued" [12]. Rio Ferdinand said the statement read "like Chat GPT" and asked for "a voice" [13][71]. Romano called it "sad" [72]. Laurie Whitwell posted that Carrick "deserves better" [73]. The Mail reports staff inside the club share Neville's view [7]. The Telegraph reports Carrick himself was "feeling sore" about how it unfolded, with "some resentment" about the leaks [74]. The MEN's Steven Railston said the club believes it followed "due process" but "the optics… were not good" [75]. Players lined up on Instagram. Harry Maguire wrote "Will always be a legend. Respect. Thank you." and Fernandes "Thanks for everything, boss." [76][77]. Mainoo, Lacey, Tyler Fletcher, Dorgu, Baleba, Heaven and Mbeumo also posted [77]. MUST said responsibility lies "with the ownership" [78][79].

WHO IS SAYING WHAT ABOUT WHETHER IT WAS RIGHT

Here the coverage splits more evenly. The Telegraph's Jason Burt: "There is no doubt that sacking Michael Carrick was the right decision, even if it may not have been handled in the right way" [17]. Sebastian Stafford-Bloor in The Athletic argued the mistake came in May, not November. Making Carrick permanent was "sentimental and hopelessly naive", because the qualities that calm a club after a bad spell are not the ones needed to rebuild it, the same mistake United made with Solskjaer [80]. His sharper point was about authority. When Lisandro Martínez said of Carrick "He does his best", he was talking as you would about a bystander [80]. Big-club management, he wrote, "is big tent preaching" [80]. The MEN's Ross Jackson listed eight steps to the end and concluded "it's time for him to go" [81]. Nick Miller's line was that he "should have been done weeks ago" [61].

On the other side, The Athletic's Chris McKenna laid out five reasons it went wrong, and the first was not Carrick's fault [82]. United spent £150m on Baleba, Tielemans and Andrey Santos, but with Casemiro gone and Manuel Ugarte injured since the World Cup that amounts to one extra midfielder. There was no back-up striker for Benjamin Sesko and nothing for a defence that leaked 50 league goals last season [82]. After that came the tactical shift to more possession, Fernandes' form, injuries, and selection, with Dalot at left-back five games running [82]. The BBC's feature claimed a source close to one player asked why so much attention went on the midfield. It also has the detail that United's back four for the first home game was the same as Ten Hag's first home game in 2022 [54]. The Telegraph said the Hull defeat on the opening day "caused more disquiet internally than was admitted at the time" [1]. Cerys Jones in The Athletic wrote that Carrick's reserved nature, "once a tonic to Amorim's explosiveness", came to be seen by some as a lack of authority, and asked how many chances the hierarchy needed to pick the right coach [24].

Schmeichel would have kept him [64]. MUST said another change of head coach changes nothing while the ownership stays as it is [78]. Neville called another change "rinse and repeat" [83]. Roy Keane, on The Overlap, said the club was "frightened" of missing the Champions League, then asked: "But who's making that decision? Business people or footballing people?" [84]. Charlie Austin on Sky Sports News: "If they don't do that, we'll be in the same scenario in 18 months' time" [63].

5. The Big Story, part two: Mauricio Pochettino (12–14 Nov)

WHAT IS CONFIRMED AND REPORTED

United have confirmed Pochettino as head coach [11]. US Soccer confirmed it from its side on Friday, at a press conference where it said he will be leaving "to take the head coaching position at Manchester United" [114]. The terms come from the reporting, not the club, unless a statement says otherwise. On Thursday night David Ornstein posted: "Mauricio Pochettino to become Manchester United boss on 2.5yr contract. Deal until summer 2029" [9]. Fabrizio Romano posted "here we go", saying US Soccer had given the green light after Pochettino agreed terms on Wednesday [10]. Sky, ESPN and The Athletic all have the same length [83][30][9].

The cost: a release clause in his USA contract of about $10m, quoted as anything from £7m to £7.5m depending on the outlet and the exchange rate, plus roughly $2m more for his staff [30][24][14]. Jesús Pérez, Miguel D'Agostino, Toni Jiménez and his son Sebastiano are all expected to come with him [30][85]. He will coach the USA in both Concacaf Nations League quarter-final legs against Haiti, in Miami on Saturday and Cincinnati on Tuesday, then fly in [29][30][31]. BBC Sport expects him at Carrington on 18 or 19 November [86]. Liverpool away is on 22 November [32].

Sky's Rob Dorsett has the timeline: first direct contact on Tuesday night alongside the approach to US Soccer. United concluded they could not prise Emery from Villa. Pochettino was "not a second choice", and there were no direct talks with Nagelsmann, Howe or Conte [87]. Ornstein, on The Athletic's podcast, said United spoke to Pochettino in 2024 and "didn't think Pochettino was the man then". He added: "It's a very expensive decision not to go with him in the summer, given they are going with him now" [88]. He extended his USA deal only in August [62][31]. Burt counts this as the fourth time: a Mayfair lunch with Ferguson in 2016, 2019, and an interview in 2022 before Ten Hag got the job [17].

Pochettino himself said very little about United at his farewell press conference with US Soccer on Friday. He said there would be time next week to talk about the job, and that for now his focus was entirely on the two Haiti games [114]. He called leaving "one of the most difficult decision in our career" and admitted: "It's true that this is not ideal" [114]. His account of the timing: the squad arrived "last Friday", the situation and "some contact" started on "Sunday afternoon", and he told the players and staff on Thursday once the agreement was in place [114]. US Soccer said the release-clause money stays with the federation, a nonprofit, to pay for coaches, youth national teams and coaching education [114].

WHO IS SAYING WHAT ABOUT HIM

Barney Ronay in the Guardian opened with "Welcome, Mauricio, to the meat grinder", then landed the line of the week: hiring him now "is like booking the bloke who did Gangnam Style to play the Super Bowl" [89]. Read on, though, and Ronay is more interested than dismissive. He calls it a "union of lost souls", broken club meets broken manager, and sees something "astringent and purging" in Pochettino's best football, with Mainoo, Baleba, Santos, Cunha and Mbeumo possibly suiting it [89]. His bigger point is the one that runs through this whole piece. In his view the hiring and firing is "a symptom not a cause" [89].

The Guardian's Andrew Beasley says Chelsea 2023-24 is the best yardstick for what to expect: 77 scored, 63 conceded, chaotic games. "He is an elite manager," he writes, but "the relevance of his recent work to United in 2026-27 is less clear" [90]. Burt expects it to be "his way or the highway" and says the squad "will get a rude awakening" from his fitness regime [17]. Toby Alderweireld, who played for him at Spurs, told Sky that United "lack a little bit of intensity" and the players face "the toughest preseason in their history" [91]. The Overlap's Breakdown called it "a good decision", with the caveat that his Chelsea and USA sides gave away more big chances than his Spurs did [92]. Paul Scholes: "a brilliant appointment … but I've got that slight concern we are not getting his best years" [63]. Neville hopes he "protects himself" on the way in [12]. Harry Kane "can't speak highly enough of him" [64]. In the USA camp, Folarin Balogun said that if he had a vote "I would like to keep things the way they are" [93].

Ian Herbert in the Mail, who has the sharpest take on who chose him (see talking point 6), still thinks there should be optimism. Of all the candidates, he writes, Pochettino has the charisma and "fundamental toughness" to put some mettle back into the place [14].

The YouTuber Stephen Howson looked at who comes in with him: Jesús Pérez, Miguel D'Agostino, goalkeeping coach Toni Jiménez and Sebastiano Pochettino [115]. He splits "hard" into two kinds. One is the physical kind, the running and the standards, where he rates Pochettino "as hard as anyone in the game". The other is the dressing-room kind, the fear of a manager who pulls you up, which he says United "haven't had really since 2013" [115]. In his view Pérez will enforce the first, but nobody on the staff does the second, and the Spurs version worked because those players were young and hungry [115]. His conclusion is aimed above the new head coach: the real test is whether Wilcox and the rest "can do the hard thing for him" and clear out the players who won't buy in [115].

6. Who is actually in charge?

This is the subplot that matters most, and the one where the reporting genuinely splits. I won't try to settle it, because nothing we have settles it. Here is who says what.

  • The Daily Mail (Ian Herbert): "Sources have told us that the Glazers wanted Pochettino and ordered Sir Jim Ratcliffe and his men to fall in line with that." He points to a link through the hedge fund billionaire Ken Griffin, whom he describes as the main contributor to Pochettino's US Soccer salary and a funder of the Glazers' 2005 takeover. Herbert reports Wilcox wanted Eddie Howe and recruitment director Christopher Vivell wanted Julian Nagelsmann, which leaves Berrada and Wilcox looking like "irrelevant also-rans" [14].
  • The Mirror: the Glazers "overruled" Wilcox and Vivell; "the Glazer family retain ultimate authority on major decisions" [94].
  • talkSPORT (via SI): the Glazers "spearheaded" the pursuit [95].
  • Sky Sports: Ratcliffe "ratified" a recommendation from Berrada and Wilcox [69].
  • The Athletic: the decision was reached in Monaco but needed "final approval" from both Ratcliffe and the Glazers [24].
  • TEAMtalk (via Football365, 10 Nov): Berrada picked Pochettino and Joel Glazer agreed; Ratcliffe favoured an English appointment [96].
  • Manchester United: deny that Pochettino is a Glazer appointee [14][94].

There are a few reasons the accounts might differ, and none of them requires anyone to be making it up. The structure lets everyone claim a hand, since INEOS runs football operations and the Glazers sign off major decisions [69][78]. Rival camps tend to brief when a decision might go wrong. And some of these stories (TEAMtalk's on 10 Nov) came before the decision, while others came after. Dorsett's "not a second choice" sits awkwardly with the Howe and Nagelsmann stories [87][14]. Kaveh Solhekol summed up the confusion: "Is it the Glazer family? Is it Ratcliffe and Ineos? Is it Berrada and Wilcox?" [97].

All of this sits on top of a lot. Avram Glazer's line about commercial power [38]. MUST's statement: "Another change of head coach cannot repair what the ownership continues to undermine" [78]. The 1958 urging fans to show "the Glazers and INEOS exactly what you think" [58]. Accounts showing record revenue of £677.6m and a £47m pre-tax loss [41]. The Guardian's Jamie Jackson puts Ratcliffe's stake at 29.4% and the debt at about £1.2bn [63]. The Mail reports that from February the Glazers can force Ratcliffe to sell his stake to a third party below the £25 a share he paid, unless he matches the offer, and that INEOS has cut 450 jobs [7]. The Telegraph has Ratcliffe and the Glazers holding crisis talks by video link on 27 October before giving Carrick until the break [1]. It also has an Amazon documentary crew filming all season, and one insider saying "There's only been one winner at United this season and it's Amazon" [1].

The columnists were blunt. Jackson called Carrick "the latest fall guy for Manchester United's dysfunctional hierarchy" and said the only real reset would be for Ratcliffe, Wilcox, Berrada and the Glazers "to accept they are the problem and depart" [63]. Jonathan Wilson: "When you've burned through a long list of managers in 13 years, it's perhaps worth considering whether the manager is really the problem" [63]. Marshall in the MEN quoted Ratcliffe's own words from February 2024: "you can't blame all the coaches… I'm not really focused on the coach". By that measure, Marshall wrote, Ratcliffe has become the problem [66]. Herbert's insider: "I haven't got a clue who can make a go of it with the people above them" [14]. Keane: "The Glazers, how will they see this?" [84].

The Mail's Riath Al-Samarrai adds a new strand: who controls transfers. He says one of Pochettino's former colleagues told him on Friday that greater control over transfers was expected to be "a key tenet" of the negotiations, after it was "a huge bone of contention" for him at Chelsea [116]. That is one unnamed source describing an expectation, not a confirmed term of the deal, and Al-Samarrai says himself that time will tell whether any promises are honoured [116]. His read is that the job is two jobs. On the pitch, getting a squad that jogged under Carrick to run. Upstairs, dealing with Wilcox and a recruitment team that, in his view, "effectively cost Carrick his job" [116]. He thinks Pochettino's PSG, Chelsea and USA spells mean he "knows the dance", and that United "may have invited their own worst enemy to dinner, or their best chance of a solution" [116]. He also has the trivia of the week: Wilcox and Pochettino shared a pitch just once, England 0-0 Argentina at Wembley in February 2000, with Wilcox playing the full 90 and Pochettino coming on with 35 minutes left and, in Al-Samarrai's words, kicking "a few people" [116]. His line on the contrast with Carrick: Pochettino "won't drive to work for three days straight while knowing his race is run" [116].

7. Bruno says no again

Late on Thursday, The Athletic (Whitwell and Crafton) and Sky Sports (Danyal Khan) separately reported that Fernandes has rejected a second, improved contract offer, after a first in the summer [15][16]. "Fernandes is not yet satisfied, so further negotiations are anticipated," The Athletic says [15]. Salary and length are the issues. The Athletic notes that Ratcliffe's regime has been trying to cut the wage bill, and TEAMtalk says INEOS are wary of his age (32) and wages [15][98]. His deal runs to June 2027 and United hold a 12-month option [16][15][99]. As things stand he can talk to foreign clubs about a free transfer from January, but The Athletic says triggering the option "would overrule that freedom", which squares with Sky's version [15][16]. The club want him to stay and he wants to continue, The Athletic adds; the question for both sides is "to what degree" [15]. beIN points out that rejecting an offer "does not necessarily mean Fernandes has decided to leave" [100]. He was the subject of major interest from Al Hilal in May 2025, and The Athletic reported in August that Galatasaray had offered €20m a year [15]. The €65m release clause that was open to foreign clubs earlier this summer has since expired [15]. Sky says he nearly joined Pochettino's Spurs in 2019, and The Athletic that Pochettino wanted to sign him for Tottenham that year [16][15].

The timing is what makes it awkward. Fernandes questioned Carrick's tactics after the 4-1 at Como, was dropped for Bournemouth and came back as captain at Chelsea [24][99]. Then, after the Roma win on 3 November, he told Amazon Prime there had been "a lot of lies around many things that have happened at this club, between me and the manager, like that we have cleared the air. Nothing like that happened" [99]. The MEN dates that quote to after Villa and cuts it before "Nothing like that happened", which changes what it means [101]. We're using the fuller version, dated 3 November. The Athletic adds that Fernandes has since said he was trying to explain that a tactical question was not his to answer, and that he did not mean to inflame things. It also reports that his relationship with Carrick was "said to be perfectly fine" after the two spoke following the Bournemouth defeat [15]. On Thursday he posted "Thanks for everything, boss" [101][77].

The Mail calls it an "early headache" for Pochettino [99]. Stafford-Bloor's piece suggests Fernandes was always going to win the stand-off with Carrick, because he is too valuable and carries the kind of weight only a much stronger coaching presence can contain [80]. McKenna notes that all of his league goal contributions this season came in two games, Ipswich and Villa [82]. The Athletic says he is still the team's best player "in the opinion of the majority at Carrington", and compares the situation to Liverpool's handling of Mohamed Salah's contract in early 2025 [15]. The Times has run a column arguing that moving on from him is the only way United will grow. We have only seen the headline [102]. Ronay wonders whether Pochettino will ask him for more discipline and less risk [89].

8. Transfers and the academy

A new head coach and a window seven weeks away means the gossip started straight away. talkSPORT, via Sky's Paper Talk, reported United and City will compete for Chelsea's Malo Gusto [103]. TEAMtalk says Chelsea value him at £75m [104]. The Sun's January shortlist names Rayan Ait-Nouri, Tyrick Mitchell and Rodrigo Muniz [105]. Ben Jacobs says "defence is the big priority" and expects United to "back their manager in the market" [106].

Stephen Howson, in a second video, picks up two names doing the rounds: Monaco striker Folarin Balogun and Fulham left-back Antonee Robinson, both of whom have played for Pochettino with the USA [117]. He relays a Fabrizio Romano report that the club have said there will be "movement" in January, and a Telegraph line that the priorities are a defender and a striker. We have not seen either original, so treat both as second-hand for now [117]. Howson's objection is not to the players, who he thinks would "raise the floor", but to what the pattern says. If the targets are the new manager's former players, and experienced ones at that, he argues it is manager-led recruitment cutting across the young-player model INEOS sold, and that promising the new man signings the old one never got is "a confession" the summer went wrong [117]. Balogun, remember, is the player who said of Pochettino leaving the USA: "If I had a vote, I would like to keep things the way they are" [93].

Looking further ahead, The Sun's Samuel Luckhurst reports that United are monitoring Como's Nico Paz ahead of a potential move next summer [118]. Paz, 22, scored twice and set one up in Como's 4-1 win over United last month, and Luckhurst says United were interested before that, with Paz entering the final 18 months of his contract [118]. The catch is Real Madrid's £67m buy-back option, which can be activated next summer [118]. The Sun frames him as a possible Fernandes successor and points to the Argentine link with Pochettino [118]. Howson had got there on his own: Paz is the player he would go for to replace Fernandes, though he thinks Madrid may still have first call and rules out January [117]. Luckhurst's piece also says United will look to sell Joshua Zirkzee next year after blocking a deadline-day move to Everton, and that a Rashford sale is "essential" in 2027 unless his form changes things [118].

In the squad Pochettino inherits, Sesko is out until at least January and Luke Shaw is the only senior left-back, and nobody was signed in the summer to cover either [54][82]. The Guardian reports Marcus Rashford is aiming at Liverpool for his return from a groin injury [28]. The Sun adds more on the gaps. Zirkzee has not started a game this season, Mbeumo has played most of it through the middle in Sesko's absence, and Amad has not scored for United since 15 December. Cunha, the natural heir at No 10, has spent most of the year on the left [118].

At the other end, JJ Gabriel's move to Real Madrid is agreed verbally but not done, with nothing official from either club [107]. The Athletic's Daniel Taylor wonders whether he really thinks it will be simpler at the Bernabéu [108]. Shea Lacey has given supporters the lift they wanted. He assisted the Roma winner and came off the bench against Villa, and Carrick said of him "We love him to bits" [109][46]. James Ducker says he has "that little glint in his eye" [110]. Where sources agree and disagree

COMMON GROUND

  • The sequence. Carrick was sacked on Thursday morning, told in person at Carrington after Wednesday's Monaco meeting. He took training on Tuesday and Wednesday while it was all going on. Every major outlet agrees, and the club statement confirms the outcome [24][6][69][70][27][63][7][111][8].
  • The handling was poor. Neville, Schmeichel, Ferdinand, Romano, Whitwell, Miller and, by the Mail's account, the club's own staff [12][64][13][72][73][61][7]. Even Burt, who backs the decision, says it "may not have been handled in the right way" [17].
  • Carrick isn't the main culprit. MUST, Jackson, Wilson, Solhekol, Cerys Jones, McKenna and the BBC all point first at the summer window and the people above the head coach [78][63][26][24][82][54].
  • Pochettino's terms. Two and a half years to summer 2029, a release clause of about $10m in a USA deal signed in August, both Haiti games first [9][83][30][31][29].
  • Defending is the problem he inherits. Neville, Unwin, Ronay, Alderweireld and Jacobs come at it from different angles and land in the same place: 30 conceded in all competitions [23][44][89][91][106].
  • He brings intensity. Burt, Alderweireld, The Overlap Breakdown, Ronay and Stephen Howson agree on what he will try to do [17][91][92][89][115]. Howson's doubt is whether intensity alone is enough for this dressing room [115].
  • United could have had him for free in the summer. Ornstein, Miller, the Mail and Herbert all say so, and nobody disputes it [88][61][31][14].

WHERE OPINIONS DIFFER

  • Who chose Pochettino. The Mail and Mirror have the Glazers overruling INEOS's football people. Sky has Ratcliffe ratifying a Berrada/Wilcox recommendation. The Athletic says both owners had to approve. TEAMtalk says Berrada drove it. United deny it was a Glazer appointment [14][94][69][24][96]. This is a disagreement about facts, and probably also about who is briefing whom.
  • Was sacking Carrick right? Yes: Burt, Stafford-Bloor, Ross Jackson, Miller [17][80][81][61]. No, or not convinced it helps: Schmeichel, MUST, and Neville's "rinse and repeat" [64][78][83]. The divide is mostly between people judging the head coach and people judging the club.
  • Is Pochettino the right man? Positive: Scholes, Alderweireld, The Overlap Breakdown, Beasley's "elite manager", Kane [63][91][92][90][64]. Sceptical: Ronay on timing, Miller (who'd prefer Conte), Scholes and Beasley on whether this is his best period [89][61][90]. The sceptics mostly doubt the timing and the club, not the man.

FACTS AND NUMBERS THAT DON'T LINE UP

  • The release clause in pounds. Every outlet has $10m. Converted, it is £7m at Sky and BBC, £7.4m at The Athletic and Telegraph, and £7.5m at ESPN, the Guardian and Herbert [69][24][30][14]. Staff costs: about $2m (ESPN), "above £10m" all in (Mail), about £14m including Carrick's pay-off (Express) [30][31][112].
  • Carrick's pay-off. "Minimal termination costs" (Sky's Dorsett) and a break clause (the Sun, via Jacobs), against about £5m with staff (Telegraph via the Express) [87][67][112].
  • When the Monaco meeting was. Tuesday (talkSPORT, the Sun, the Guardian on Monday night) or Wednesday (BBC, Sky, MEN). It was Wednesday [23][25][60][6][26].
  • Who told Carrick. Berrada alone (The Athletic), or Berrada and Wilcox (Telegraph, MEN, Mail, AP) [24][27][113][7][111].
  • Bruno's "lies" quote. After Roma on 3 November with "Nothing like that happened" (Mail), or after Villa and shorter (MEN) [99][101].
  • Can Bruno talk to clubs in January? Less of a split than it first looked. The Athletic's full piece says he can as things stand, but that United's option "would overrule that freedom" if triggered, which is Sky's point too. The Mail's headline leaves the option out [15][16][99].
  • Carrick's games and record. 15 or 16 games as permanent head coach, 10 or 11 months in charge, depending on how interim and pre-season are counted [113][24][27][63].
  • Villa minute marks and stats. Fernandes' penalty at 13 minutes (won) or 16 (scored). Goretzka on 53 or 54. 26 shots (Guardian) or 28 (MEN). Different data providers and the VAR check explain most of it [3][43][45][50].
  • How many times United passed on Pochettino. Three (Telegraph: 2016, 2019, 2022) or twice (Mail: 2018 and 2022). It partly depends whether a lunch counts [17][14].
  • The Ferguson dinner. Saturday night (Telegraph) or Friday night (Mail) [1][7].
  • When the first contact came. Pochettino says the situation, and "some contact", started on the Sunday afternoon, the day of the Villa game; Sky's Dorsett has the first direct contact on Tuesday night [114][87]. Pochettino did not say who that contact was with, so the two may not be describing the same thing.

WHAT IS STILL UNKNOWN

  • Who actually picked Pochettino, and whether Ratcliffe or the Glazers will ever say. An opinion growing among journalist reports suggest that the Glazers overruled Wilcox.
  • Why it took three days. Nobody at the club has explained it beyond "due process" [27].
  • What Carrick's pay-off cost, which should show in the next accounts.
  • Whether United trigger Bruno's option before January, and whether a third offer comes.
  • Whether there is January money, and how much.
  • Whether Pochettino really has more control over transfers, as Al-Samarrai's source expects, and what that leaves for Wilcox [116].
  • Anything Carrick himself says. He has not spoken publicly in anything we have seen.

The United Roundup view

Opinion

It’s not something I feel good about saying, but - I think sacking Michael was the right call, as much as I hated how they did it. You just can't be 18th with one league win since August and keep the head coach on goodwill alone, and Jason Burt is right about that. But Gary Neville is right too. Letting him drive past the cameras for two days while the bosses flew to Monaco and lined up his successor wasn't "due process". It looked like a process built around Monaco rather than around a person and owners that aren’t even resident in the country the club they own plays in. The process certainly didn’t consider Michael Carrick's feelings or how this terribly handled dismissal reflects on the club. It's a poor way to treat the one person everybody at the club still likes, and a man who played 464 games for us.

What bothers me more is that the thing that got him the sack wasn't really him. Yes he could’ve tried different things tactically or made different team selections or substitutions but most agree that he probably couldn’t have changed things enough to turn around a slide that made a decision concerning his job, inevitable. No left-back, no striker, Dalot at left-back five games running, a goalkeeper whose confidence has gone, and £150-odd million on a midfield. Carrick was in charge of coaching, not recruitment so it was never his job to replace players. The review that sacked Michael wasn't much of a review because it didn't look at the people who built this squad, or the decisions that led to such a weak and shallow squad.

On Pochettino, I'm more optimistic than some. The man has wanted this job for ten years, which is a good start. It means we still have the appeal as a club. From what I have read, Poch will make them run, and the Spurs side he built on not much money played the football we keep saying we want. But let's not kid ourselves. We could have had him for nothing in the summer and we're paying seven million-odd to get him now. And if the Mail is right that the Glazers picked him over the heads of the football people, then he arrives with exactly the same problem as everyone since Fergie: nobody can tell you who's really in charge.

My view is this: It’s time to reset the culture at the club. Firstly, we can only do this if the manager gets results - and he’s just not going to get the input and application from our crop of aging and underworked players that he demands for his style of play. That’s a good thing. I know we all want to mention the effects of our poorly steered club but ownership and board aside - unless there is an overhaul of the squad over the course of the next 12 to 18 months then we will be in no better position this time next season in my opinion. I hope that Poch will have a say and be able to get the players he needs in the coming transfer windows and continue his philosophy of trusting youth and signing talented young players. He has a reputation for not suffering fools and for being able to handle big egos, which we sorely need at United. We have too many older figures that have seen too many managers come and go. I think this has given the likes of Bruno Fernandes, Harry Maguire, Luke Shaw and Lisandro Martinez a sense of entitlement and security due to always being stretched thin when it comes to positional cover and also in the transfer market.

So the bottom line is cautious hope, with a lot of fingers crossed. We have some young blood that is eager to prove itself, we have an idea of the profiles of players we need from the market, we know who needs to leave and we know some of Pochettino's methods and demands. Give him time, give him a left-back and striker in January, and for heaven's sake give him one clear boss. Anfield isn't the test. Christmas and post-January are.
